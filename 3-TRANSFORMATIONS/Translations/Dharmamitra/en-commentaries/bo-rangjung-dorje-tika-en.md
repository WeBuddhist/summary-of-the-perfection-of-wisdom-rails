# The Ocean of Perfection: A Commentary on the Verse Summary of the Perfection of Wisdom, Like a Wish-Fulfilling Jewel ^0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^0-1]]

Here is the Noble Commentary on the Verse Summary of the Perfection of Wisdom, entitled "The Ocean of Perfection," which is like a wish-fulfilling precious jewel. ^0-1

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^0-2]]

I bow to the feet of the great lion, the Subduer, who possesses the Vajra-body formed from the splendor of the mountain of the two accumulations, whose speech proclaims the sound of the sixty branches like the voice of Brahmā, and whose mind is the Victorious One, profound, vast, and difficult to fathom. I bow to the lineage gurus who realized the meaning of the supreme Perfection of Wisdom, the wondrous essence from among all the oceans of the scriptural collections of the vast and profound intent of the Victorious One, which is like the sky. From the eighty-four thousand holy teachings of the supreme vehicle, the path traversed by all the Sugatas of the three times, this essence—the Perfection of Wisdom, which is like a precious jewel—was not understood by those who previously desired to be learned, and thus this supreme meaning of the supreme vehicle was not found. Therefore, I, the bodhisattva Rang-byung-rdo-rje, who am like them, shall write down these supreme words of meaning without error, regarding this very supreme meaning that is so difficult to find.
Regarding this, there are two ways of explaining the Sūtra of the Perfection of Wisdom. This is the explanation of the yogi Kusulupa, which teaches the meaning correctly by relying upon experiential practice. Regarding that, the *Verse Summary of the Precious Qualities* teaches a summary of the general and specific characteristics as a preliminary. To begin with, the perfectly enlightened Buddha generated the mind for awakening, accumulated the two accumulations over three countless eons in the interim, and finally became perfectly enlightened. Until the cycle of existence is emptied, he performs the inconceivable and spontaneously present benefit of sentient beings without interruption.
If one asks how all the teachings spoken by the Teacher can be summarized, they are included in two: the teachings of scripture (*āgama*) and the teachings of realization (*adhigama*). As the *Abhidharmakośa* says: "The Teacher’s holy teaching is twofold, consisting of scripture and realization." The teachings of scripture are also twofold, included in the Buddha-word (*buddhavacana*) and the treatises (*śāstra*). As the *Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra* says: "All teachings are included in the Buddha-word and the treatises; the well-spoken word and its commentaries."
In this context, regarding the Buddha-word, one should know it through three aspects: the referents of the Buddha-word, the characteristics of the Buddha-word, and the classification of the examples of the Buddha-word. Regarding the first, there are three: the Buddha-word is desired because of the statement, "Listen," and so forth. Alternatively, the second refers to any speech that is directly spoken by the Buddha himself, through which an inconceivable number of scriptural collections are taught.
Regarding the first, there are three divisions based on time: the time of the first turning of the wheel of Dharma, the intermediate, and the final. Although there are an inconceivable number of ways to turn the wheel of Dharma, the first Buddha-word is called the turning of the wheel of Dharma on the four noble truths, which are the scriptural collections of the Vinaya. The intermediate Buddha-word is the turning of the wheel of Dharma on the absence of characteristics, which, among the scriptural collections, is the scriptural collection of the Sūtras. The final Buddha-word is the turning of the wheel of Dharma on the definitive meaning, which is the scriptural collection of the Abhidharma.
Regarding these, because they are the Buddha-word, there are three: the Buddha-word spoken by his own mouth, the Buddha-word blessed by him, and the Buddha-word authorized by him. The first is called the Buddha-word spoken by the mouth of the Bhagavan, the Lord of Subduers himself. The second is called the Buddha-word even when sound is produced from a piece of wood or a pebble through the blessing of the Bhagavan himself. The third is when the Tathāgata gives authorization to his retinue, such as the eight great noble Śrāvakas, or others such as the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, saying, "Speak," and their speech also counts as the Buddha-word.
Regarding the Buddha-word, there are two divisions based on what is expressed: the Buddha-word of provisional meaning and the Buddha-word of definitive meaning. The former, such as the Vinaya, is called the Buddha-word of provisional meaning. The latter, such as the *Śatasāhasrikā* and others, the Perfections of Wisdom, is called the Buddha-word of definitive meaning. Divided by the means of expression, there are the twelve branches of scripture, such as the Sūtras. Divided by superior and inferior, there are two: the Buddha-word of the superior Great Vehicle and the Buddha-word of the inferior Lesser Vehicle.
Divided by the means of antidotes, the three baskets are taught: the scriptural collection of the Vinaya as an antidote to desire, the scriptural collection of the Sūtras as an antidote to hatred, and the scriptural collection of the Abhidharma as an antidote to ignorance. The eighty-four thousand gateways to the Dharma that arise from these are taught as antidotes to the eighty-four thousand afflictions.
Divided by cause and result, there are two: the Buddha-word of the Sūtras, which makes the cause the path, and the Buddha-word of the Tantras, which makes the result the path. The view that makes the cause the path relies upon the five perfections, and there is a tradition that seeks the Perfection of Wisdom. As this very Perfection of Wisdom says: "Dedicate generosity, discipline, patience, and likewise diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom to awakening." The tradition that makes the result the path holds that the five perfections are accomplished by relying upon wisdom itself.
Having thus set forth merely the general characteristics, what are its own divisions? Among the three types of Buddha-word, this is the Buddha-word spoken by his own mouth, spoken in a single breath. Regarding what is expressed, it is of definitive meaning. Regarding the means of expression, it is the verse section. Among the superior and inferior, it is the superior. Among the three antidotes, it is the Sūtra collection. Among the two of cause and result, regarding the three times of the cause, it is the intermediate Buddha-word, the turning of the wheel of Dharma on the absence of characteristics, which is a summary of its own characteristics. That is what teaches the Perfection of Wisdom. ^0-2

In the language of India: *Āryaprajñāpāramitāsañcayagāthā*. In the language of Tibet: *’phags pa shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa sdud pa tshigs su bcad pa*. Homage to the Noble Mañjuśrī! It is stated thus. The first part, from "In the language of India" up to "Homage," constitutes the perfect and complete title. If one analyzes the Indian language, there are four types of languages; among these four, the most excellent is the Sanskrit language of the gods. Regarding this, in the context of this text, "India" (*rgya gar*) refers to the "India" of the five poisons, which are the afflictions. "Gar" refers to the fact that one is "danced" (or moved) by them. As for "language" (*skad*), because it is more excellent than all other types of languages, it is called the Sanskrit language of the gods. If one asks what that Sanskrit language of the gods is, it is the *Āryaprajñāpāramitāsañcayagāthā*. If one asks what the purpose of stating this is, it is to state the purpose of placing the Indian language at the beginning, the purpose of establishing the authenticity of the Indian language, and the purpose of leaving a karmic imprint of the Sanskrit language of the gods. Regarding the "Tibetan language," it is explained by translating it from that very Sanskrit language of the gods. If one asks how it is translated, it is from the meaning of the title up to the very end. If one translates the characteristics of the title, *ārya* means "noble," *prajñā* means "wisdom," *pāramitā* means "perfection," *sañcaya* means "summary," and *gāthā* means "verses." When these are combined, it is called the "Noble Perfection of Wisdom, the Summary of Verses." Regarding this, there is the classification by the manner of bestowing a title and the identification of the specific title bestowed. In general, the reasons for bestowing a title are: naming it after an analogy, such as the *Sūtra of the Rice Seedling*; naming it after the length, such as the *Hundred Thousand* or the *Twenty Thousand*; naming it after the chapters, such as the *Eight Thousand*; naming it after the fascicles, such as the *Bam-po* or the *Path*; naming it after the location, such as the *Laṅkāvatāra*; naming it after the author, such as the *Pramāṇavārttika* of Dharmakīrti; or naming it after the requester, such as the *Questions of Brahmā* or the *Questions of the Heroic Progress*. This text is named after its meaning. Now, the meaning of this title is explained: "Noble" (*’phags pa*) means that because one has abandoned the faults such as the five poisons and possesses perfect qualities, one is noble. "Wisdom" (*shes rab*) means that it is superior to others; knowing the relative as like an illusion and knowing the ultimate as it is, is wisdom. "Perfection" (*pha rol tu phyin pa*) means that having abandoned the perception of the limits of cyclic existence, one has gone to the other shore. "Summary" (*sdud pa*) means that it gathers all the precious qualities, like a magnet. "Verses" (*tshigs su bcad pa*) means that it is not prose, but rather the "vajra-like verses" of the faculties, consisting of eleven syllables. If others say that there is no certainty regarding the meaning of the title, there is indeed certainty, for it is stated in this very text: "The name is the summary of the precious qualities, the path to enlightenment; / This path is explained so that all sentient beings may attain it." If one asks what the purpose is of stating the Indian language, the Tibetan language, the meaning of the title, and so forth, it is to remember the kindness of the translators, to know how to read the phonetic sounds, and if one has accumulated many such instances of stating the Indian language at the beginning, there is also the purpose of knowing translation. Up to this point is the summary of the meaning of the title. Even if some say that this is an interpolation by the translators, it is a repetition of what was stated by some of the earlier masters. "Homage to the Noble Mañjuśrī Kumārabhūta" is stated because he is the *iṣṭadevatā* of the translators and scholars who are in accord with the subject matter, and therefore homage is paid. ^0-3

## The perfect narrative context as the cause for the arising of the sutra. ^1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^1-1]]

From the phrase "From then on" up to the phrase "it was taught," the phrase "From then on" refers to the time after the *Śatasāhasrikā*, the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, and so forth, had been taught. "Vanquished" refers to the four types of Māras and all defilements. These are the Māra of the divine child, the Māra of defilements, the Māra of the Lord of Death, and the Māra of the aggregates, and so forth, which have been vanquished. "Endowed" means endowed with the six qualities, such as sovereignty and so forth. Regarding this, it is said:
Glory, fame, and primordial wisdom,
Sovereignty, and excellent form,
And perfect diligence—
Possessing these six is called "endowed."
Thus, the Bhagavan is of a nature that is entirely devoid of the latent tendencies of conceptualization that are the pure "suchness" of sovereignty and the rest; and because he effortlessly strives for the benefit of self and others, he is also said to be endowed with the five types of primordial wisdom. What is meant by being endowed with fame and glory is the primordial wisdom of equality, because he is endowed with that which has become the glory of all sentient beings. Sovereignty and excellent form are to be understood as the mirror-like primordial wisdom, because they are the very source of primordial wisdom other than that. The primordial wisdom that is individual discernment is the primordial wisdom of discernment, and diligence becomes the primordial wisdom of accomplishing activities through the perfection of wisdom itself. Because of that very fact, and because "endowed" and the others are also "endowed," "passed beyond" means having passed beyond the cycle of existence or the eighty-four thousand defilements symbolized by it. Given that this is the case, one might ask: what kind of audience was he relying upon? In order to bring great joy to the fourfold assembly—gods, humans, asuras, laymen and laywomen, and monks and nuns—at the time when he was teaching the perfection of wisdom and the other six perfections, he taught and instructed these three hundred verses of the *Vajracchedikā*. ^1-1

The five are: urging the chief of the retinue to listen to the Dharma; the teacher establishing his own buddhahood through teaching the Dharma by way of examples and meaning; how to train in the six perfections; the nature of the Dharma as demonstrated by examples; and summarizing the meaning of what the teacher has taught. ^2-2

It is said, from the words "with joy" up to the words "you should listen." First, the Blessed One himself, having called upon the fourfold retinue—those who have entered into working for the benefit of sentient beings, having transcended the stains of the six defilements, namely attachment, anger, pride, ignorance, views, and doubt, after having cleared away the obscurations, and having cleared away the seven [hindrances], namely sleep, dullness, agitation, regret, and malice, having abandoned obstacles and distractions, and having established the supreme faith in this very perfection of wisdom, and having great joy and respect for the Dharma and the Teacher with a mind that listens to the Dharma in this way—urges those fourfold retinue to listen, saying, "This very meaning of the perfection of wisdom, which is certain, is this perfection itself, for whatever is the conduct of the bodhisattvas, that too is this perfection of wisdom; therefore, you should listen." ^2-3

### Teaching the Dharma by way of examples and meaning. ^2-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-4]]

It is said, the example is the mention of the "Jambudvīpa" and so forth. This Jambudvīpa is where the four rivers—the Gaṅgā, the Pakṣu, the Sindhu, and the Sītā—flow, along with five hundred tributaries, and countless other such rivers. By their power, they generate all the flowers, fruits, medicinal plants, and forests, all of which exist in dependence upon that King of Nāgas who dwells in Lake Anavatapta. Therefore, they arise from the power, splendor, and merit of that Lord of Nāgas. Just like that example, all the Dharma that the śrāvakas, such as Subhūti, teach, explain in detail, and express to other sentient beings with pleasant words through logical reasoning, as well as the attainment of the happiness of realizing nirvāṇa, which is supreme and exalted in the cycle of existence, and the attainment of its fruits—the fruit of a stream-enterer, the fruit of a once-returner, the fruit of a non-returner, and the fruit of an arhat—all of these arise from the power of that person, the Tathāgata. Why is that? Because regarding the way the Victor has turned the wheel of the Dharma of selflessness, the great śrāvakas, such as the eight supreme ones who have become the disciples of the Buddha, the supreme among men, have directly trained in it, and having directly realized the path and the fruit, they have taught it extensively and correctly to others just as they themselves have trained. If one wonders by whose power this arises, it arises from the power of the blessings of the Teacher, the Buddha himself; it does not arise from the power of those śrāvakas and so forth. Why is that? Because the Victor has fully realized the wisdom of selflessness, and because he has attained the ultimate, it arises from that. ^2-4

### Teaching the manner of training in the six perfections. ^2-3-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-5]]

When considering the meaning of these and other such passages, there are three points: the nature of the six perfections, the sequence of their practice, and the result of that practice. The first of these has three parts: the individual essence of the six perfections, the certainty of their number, and the certainty of their order.
Regarding the first of these, there are three parts. In terms of what is to be defined: *dānapāramitā* is called "generosity" because it acts to eliminate poverty. Its defining characteristic is a mind free from attachment. Its specific classifications are the giving of material goods, the giving of the Dharma, and the giving of protection from fear.
The second, in terms of what is to be defined, is *śīlapāramitā*; it is called "ethical discipline" because it signifies obtaining coolness from the burning of the afflictions. Its defining characteristic is a mind turned away from misconduct, including its seeds. Its specific classifications are the ethical discipline of restraint, the ethical discipline of gathering virtuous qualities, and the ethical discipline of benefiting sentient beings.
The third, in terms of what is to be defined, is *kṣāntipāramitā*; it is called "patience" because it signifies the patience to endure anger. Its defining characteristic is the ability to withstand. Its specific classifications are the patience of accepting suffering, the patience of being indifferent to harm, and the patience of conviction in the Dharma.
The fourth, in terms of what is to be defined, is *vīryapāramitā*; it is the diligence of engaging in supreme virtues. Its defining characteristic is enthusiasm for virtue. Its specific classifications are the diligence of armor, the diligence of engagement, and the diligence of not being satisfied.
The fifth, in terms of what is to be defined, is *samādhipāramitā* and *dhyānapāramitā*; it is called "meditative concentration" because it signifies holding the mind. Its defining characteristic is one-pointedness of focus. Its specific classifications are the meditative concentration of abiding in happiness, the meditative concentration of manifesting virtues, and the meditative concentration of engaging in the purpose of meditative concentration.
The sixth, in terms of what is to be defined, is *prajñāpāramitā*; it is called "wisdom" because it signifies knowing the ultimate truth. Its defining characteristic is the culmination of the supreme intellect that realizes the ultimate. Its specific classifications are divided according to their causal conditions: the wisdom of hearing, the wisdom of reflection, and the wisdom of meditation.
Second, regarding the certainty of their number, it is based on the necessity of what is to be accomplished: the first three accomplish the higher realms, while the latter three accomplish the definitive good. Furthermore, among the three trainings, the first is the training in ethical discipline, the latter two are the trainings in meditative concentration and wisdom, and diligence acts as a companion to both. Also, in terms of benefiting oneself and others, the first three are for one's own benefit, the latter two are for the benefit of others, and diligence acts as a companion to both; thus, the number is fixed at six.
Third, regarding the certainty of their order, there are three ways to view it. In terms of the order of cause and effect: through generosity, one gathers resources, which leads to the ethical discipline of being detached from desire; through that, one turns away from harm and abides in the four qualities of a renunciant, from which patience arises; being endowed with such patience, the diligence of manifesting virtue arises; being endowed with diligence, the meditative concentration of placing the mind one-pointedly on an object arises; and from the mind thus abiding, the wisdom of *vipaśyanā* that realizes selflessness arises. Furthermore, there is the order of inferior and superior, as it is said that ethical discipline is superior to generosity. Also, there is the order of subtle and coarse, as ethical discipline is more subtle than generosity, and so on; thus, it is determined in that way.
Now, this is the very manner in which the six perfections are taught. This should also be applied to their respective contexts accordingly. ^2-5

###### Identifying the individual who practices the perfection of wisdom. ^2-4-1-1-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-6]]

"By these methods it is taught," it is said. When it comes to the meaning of phrases such as "that for which the supreme perfection is not apprehended," if we take these as the basis, the Protector Maitreya has condensed them into eight categories. Furthermore, if we take the perspective of practice, he has taught the meaning of the perfection of wisdom, the practice, and the distinctions of the individual, and so forth, within the contexts of the knowledge of the basis, the knowledge of the path, and omniscience. Among the four types of application to the path—the context of the complete realization of all aspects, the peak, the serial, and the momentary enlightenment—the phenomena to be practiced are taught, and ultimately, the fruit is taught in the context of the dharmakāya. What are those eight? According to the *Abhisamayālaṅkāra*:
The perfection of wisdom is
Rightly explained through eight topics:
Omniscience, knowledge of the path,
Then knowledge of the basis,
Complete realization of all aspects,
The peak, the serial,
Momentary complete enlightenment,
And the dharmakāya—these are the eight.
Regarding this, the ultimate purpose of accomplishing wisdom is the omniscience that is the essence of fully determining all aspects of cause and effect, such as the generation of bodhicitta. As explained, these are also taught by these subdivisions:
Generation of bodhicitta, instructions,
The four limbs of definite discrimination,
The reliance upon practice,
The nature of the dharmadhātu,
Objects, purpose,
The activity of armor and engagement,
And the accumulation of definite emergence—
These are the Sage’s omniscience.
Although the meaning of these is explained in detail in the *Prajñāpāramitā*, here, because the Blessed One himself has condensed and taught the realizations of the perfection of wisdom, it is said that these are included incidentally, and they are not explained in detail through individual differentiation. Having stated "that for which wisdom," and so forth, it means that the supreme perfection of wisdom in which the bodhisattva practices is not apprehended as two—as appearance and as truth—and likewise, the practitioner, the bodhisattva himself, is not apprehended, and the bodhicitta signified by that is also not apprehended. ^2-6

###### The manner in which that individual attains the fruition of the Dharma. ^2-4-1-1-2-0

###### Attaining enlightenment through non-referentiality. ^2-4-1-1-2-1-0

###### Attainment through the non-referentiality of objects. ^2-4-1-1-2-1-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-8]]

From "without form" up to "abiding in wisdom": In reality, the five aggregates do not exist as form consisting of the colors and shapes of true appearances. Likewise, there is no feeling, no perception that grasps at signs and experiences, and no volition of mental formations. For a bodhisattva practicing from such wisdom, there is not even an atom of a true basis for consciousness. Similarly, because they do not abide in any of the phenomena of cyclic existence that are afflicted, nor in any of the phenomena of nirvana that are purified, they abide in the ultimate without abiding. Thus, the Sugatas attained enlightenment by practicing a meditative absorption in which all phenomena are not grasped. For example, just as in the past, a wandering ascetic who knew the crafts, though free from previous desires, had not attained the liberation that includes defilement, yet through the intellect that explains emptiness, there arose a state where the object of the wisdom that knows the non-conceptual aggregates was seen as nothing at all, destroying everything; in the same way, a bodhisattva who knows that a wandering ascetic does not perceive the phenomena of affliction in that manner, and who also knows that according to the Śrāvakas this is nirvana, yet does not touch upon it—that is what is meant by "abiding in wisdom." ^2-8

###### Attainment through the non-referentiality of the subject. ^2-4-1-1-2-1-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-9]]

It is said: From "this perfection of wisdom" up to "is near to awakening": Furthermore, this perfection of wisdom that a bodhisattva should practice—whose is it, what is its essence, who is the person, and from what object did it arise?—by discerning this, one realizes that all these phenomena of the afflicted cyclic existence and the purified phenomena of nirvana are empty. Having investigated in this way, a bodhisattva who listens to the teachings of the Great Vehicle and practices them without shrinking, without fear, and without terror, is near to the perfection of wisdom, which is itself unsurpassed awakening. ^2-9

###### Reversal. ^2-4-1-1-2-2-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-11]]

From "If, while lacking realization" up to "lacks faith": If one engages with the aggregates—perceiving form as truly existent while lacking realization of the suchness of phenomena, and likewise engaging with feeling, volition, and consciousness—then one is engaging with signs and lacks faith in the state of non-arising. Similarly, a bodhisattva who holds that the emptiness of these five aggregates is something that has become an actual entity through the negation of them is one who engages with the signs of the object to be negated and the negating phenomenon; they do not have faith in the state of naturally non-arising. ^2-11

###### Authenticity ^2-4-1-1-2-2-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-12]]

It is said: From "not the form of a person" up to "is predicted": However, one who does not engage in form as truly appearing in the ultimate sense, and likewise does not engage in feeling, does not engage in perception, does not engage in volition, and does not engage in consciousness as truly appearing, but rather engages without abiding in anything—this is called "practicing the self," and because of a firm wisdom that knows not to abide in what is called "not practicing." Through this, one attains the supreme meaning of non-arising, and by possessing intelligence, one also attains meditative absorptions such as the "heroic stride," and by touching the supreme peace, one engages in the perfection of wisdom in the ultimate sense. A bodhisattva who practices in this way, and who thus abides in peace, holding this to be the meditative absorption of the self, is one to whom the tathāgatas, the perfect and complete buddhas of the past, have given a prediction of awakening. ^2-12

###### Knowing the ultimate as non-dual ^2-4-1-1-2-3-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-14]]

Regarding the meaning from "The self" up to "definitely emerges": "The self is settled in the nature of phenomena" means there is no conceit that "now I am rising from that." Why is that? Because they have thoroughly understood the nature of phenomena—such as what is to be cultivated and the cultivator—as emptiness, in that the two—the phenomena of cyclic existence which are defilements, and the phenomena of nirvāṇa which are purification—cannot be expressed as "this is cyclic existence" or "this is nirvāṇa." If one practices in that manner, without inferiority or conceit, one is practicing the perfection of wisdom of the sugatas. A bodhisattva who practices in this way, because they thoroughly understand that the very fact of not practicing in anything is the practice of a bodhisattva, does not become one who apprehends the phenomena in which they practice as truly appearing; this non-apprehension is practicing the meaning of the supreme perfection of wisdom. In general, since any phenomenon of defilement or purification does not exist in reality, it is also not existent in conventional terms; just as one enters into that, childish ordinary beings conceptually construct them, distinguish them as existent or nonexistent, and become attached. In reality, both this construction of existence and non-existence are the nature of phenomena devoid of pristine wisdom. Thus, in accordance with the way things are, any bodhisattva who truly knows this perfection of wisdom—this phenomenon that has transcended both the extreme of existence and the extreme of non-existence—by knowing this, will definitely emerge from cyclic existence. ^2-14

For example, just as when a causal seed is planted and remains in the earth, the resultant sprout arises, and once the collection of causes and conditions is complete, the many forms of the sprout are distinctly produced; in the same way, all the qualities of the five perfections at the time of the cause, and all the qualities of the three enlightenments at the time of the result, arise from the perfection of wisdom that does not conceptualize the three spheres. Again, for example, just as from whatever path a universal monarch travels, there proceed the seven precious jewels such as the wheel, as well as horses, infantry, soldiers, and the retinue of the army—the fourfold army—in the same way, from the very path of the perfection of wisdom, which the Victors have realized and taught, all phenomena of the qualities of abandonment and realization will proceed. ^2-56

So it is said: from "the recognition of difference" up to "not relying on others," the response is given. Being free from the recognition that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are truly different, they speak words of truth that are consistent with their lineage, and they do not rely upon other non-Buddhist ascetics or brahmins as their refuge. Being skilled in cause and effect, they always abandon non-virtuous causes, and thus they also abandon the three lower realms as their result. By relying on the qualities of asceticism, they do not desire gain, and having become the best of men, they always desire the Dharma of the Great Vehicle. They transcend the domain of the māras of inferiority and arrogance, and they do not rely on other guides. It is said: from the phrase "in the four concentrations" up to the phrase "one should know these to be the case," [the meaning is as follows]: although the mind abides in the four concentrations, one does not abide in those concentrations in a manner that experiences their warmth; because one understands all sounds to be like echoes, one does not desire praise or fame; because the mental continuum is not bound or tied by the mind of anger, there is no [affliction]; even if a bodhisattva becomes a householder for the sake of others, although they always utilize the objects of desire, they are not attached to them because they do not crave any of the substances of desire; for the sake of sustaining life, they do not live by means of wealth that involves abandoning [virtue] such as stealing or begging; they do not engage in violent conduct such as striking or fighting; they do not recite mantras that cause harm; they do not perform mantras for the purpose of subjugating women; and they do not predict that those who desire to be served will give birth to sons or daughters—they do not make such predictions of becoming male or female, just as Subhūti did not make a prediction for the householder. From "taught by the Buddha" up to "will attain the supreme," the meaning is as follows: If one asks, "If the reality of phenomena (dharmatā) is without increase or decrease, then it is illogical that merit should increase through meditation," [the answer is that] the attainment of awakening through the purification of adventitious stains upon that which is naturally pure is taught only as a summary in the Buddha's sutras; it will be explained in detail through the obstacles and the means of accomplishment. It is said: Furthermore, any person of little intelligence who, due to the distinction of physical seclusion, dwells for many millions of years in mountain clefts five hundred leagues away from villages, in places filled with scorpions, snakes, birds, and wild beasts, yet does not know that this is not seclusion—because their mind is not secluded from the realization of existence and quiescence—is a bodhisattva of little intelligence who, having attained superior conceit through mere physical seclusion, dwells in such a state of what should be abandoned. Regarding the passage "Just as the perfection of wisdom is empty of characteristics," it means that the perfection of wisdom is emptiness, or alternatively, that the perfection of wisdom is empty of defining marks and characteristics, because it is empty of the basis of characteristics. It is said: From the passage "All these phenomena..." up to "...is the practice," it means that, in the same way, all these phenomena—comprising the afflicted cycle of existence and the purified state of nirvāṇa—should be known as empty, just like the characteristics of the perfection of wisdom. ###### The thorough affliction arising from that. ^2-4-1-3-3-4-3-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-212]]

It is said: From the passage "Sentient beings..." up to "...the afflictions are taught," if one asks, "If all phenomena are empty, how can there be the two categories of the afflicted cycle of existence and the purified state of nirvāṇa?" it is because sentient beings, through their complete misunderstanding of grasping at a self, desire the food of sensory objects. Possessing a mind attached to the self and what belongs to the self—which are the causes of the cycle of existence—they are incited by the afflictions of that self and, accumulating karma from them, they cycle perpetually. However, the self and the phenomena of "what belongs to the self" to which they are attached do not exist in reality and are empty. Ordinary childish beings grasp at what is non-existent as if it were existent, which is like trying to tie a knot in empty space. Similarly, for example, when someone takes poison due to a mistaken perception, even though that poison has not actually entered into their physical substance, they will still be overcome by it. In the same way, ordinary childish beings who claim there is a self and what belongs to the self, because they perceive them as such, are born and die due to the power of grasping at them, despite their being truly non-existent. In short, it is taught that the extent to which there is grasping at a self and what belongs to the self is the extent to which there is the afflicted cycle of existence. ^2-212

From the passage "In the Jambudvīpa..." up to "...should engage," the great benefit of meditating in that way is shown by means of an analogy. ###### Incidental accumulation ^2-4-1-3-3-5-3-3-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-224]]

It is said: From the passage "whenever a bodhisattva" up to "learning in this teaching of the Buddha," it means that at whatever time some bodhisattvas engage in physical conflict, verbal dispute, and harbor mutually discordant and angry minds toward one another, at that time, Māra and his retinue become joyful, their bodies feel supremely at ease, and they think: "These two bodhisattvas who are fighting and disputing are becoming far removed from the wisdom of the Victors, so my own purpose will be accomplished," and they become mentally delighted. Regarding the actual situation, those two bodhisattvas become distant from wisdom and their causes become fierce and predatory; thus, both of them fall away from the vow of the mind-generation they had previously made. If one asks how they fall away: since that intention is hateful and devoid of patience, how could there be awakening? There is none. At such a time, Māra and his faction become extremely joyful. In particular, if any bodhisattva who has not yet received a prophecy begins to feel angry and starts a dispute with a bodhisattva who has previously received a prophecy, because they are possessed of the fault of the ash of defilement, they must don the armor of patience for as many eons as the number of such moments of thought. The antidote to that negativity, or the method to reverse it, is patience; for all the Buddhas have also attained the supreme awakening by cultivating the perfection of patience. Therefore, thinking "this hateful mind is not good," one should generate the mindfulness of regret, confess individually through various antidotes, restrain such thoughts and actions in the future, and not take pleasure in what was previously done; this is what is meant by learning in this teaching of the Buddha. ^2-224

Having completely abandoned the perception of the truth of wisdom and the perception of the truth of sentient beings, by proceeding on the path of the Great Vehicle, they are said to abide in the bodhisattva's perfection of ethical discipline. ^2-298

###### Benefits ^2-4-2-4-3-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-299]]

Thus it is said. From the passage beginning with "If a bodhisattva" up to "spoken of by the Guide," the faults and qualities of that very subject are explained: if a bodhisattva, through a lack of skill in means, practices the path of the Victorious One, and—thinking, "These are possessed of ethical discipline, and these are transgressors of ethical discipline"—clings to the meaning of signs, then because of a view of self, they enter into a perception of truth regarding various distinct things. This is a great transgression of ethical discipline in the Great Vehicle, for their vow-based ethical discipline is impaired, and for one who holds to it in that way, that ethical discipline of the three spheres of purity is absent. Conversely, for a person who lacks the perception of permanence, the perception of a self, and the perception of the truth of sentient beings, and who is free from the desire of perceiving distinct truths, the cause for transgression is exhausted; so how could it be said that they lack the vow? It does not exist. Therefore, for anyone who knows all phenomena to be of an equal nature, there is no conceit of "vow" or "non-vow," and this lack of conceit is what the Guides—that is, the fully enlightened Buddhas—have called the bodhisattva's vow of ethical discipline. Up to this point, the meaning of the perfection of ethical discipline has been analyzed and explained. ^2-299

##### Showing the meaning of the perfection of generosity ^2-4-2-5-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-300]]

Thus it is said. ^2-300

###### Emptiness of non-attachment through being embraced by wisdom ^2-4-2-5-1-0

###### Following ^2-4-2-5-1-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-301]]

From "a pure mind" up to "there is no basis": furthermore, regarding any sentient being whose continuum is completely purified, they possess a morality that is free from such arrogance, and they give away objects of generosity without viewing pleasant or unpleasant things with attachment or aversion. They give away internal things such as their head and limbs, and they have no mind that shrinks from giving. They give away all external things and have no attachment to what has been given. Having understood that internal and external phenomena—the defilements of cyclic existence and the purified phenomena of nirvāṇa—are by nature selfless, unreal, illusory, and without essence, the bodhisattva has no mind that shrinks or is unable to give away even their own flesh. If that is absent, then it goes without saying that there is no shrinking from giving away external objects. If one realizes that the basis is selfless, then to be stingy about it is to have no basis. ^2-301

###### Reversing ^2-4-2-5-1-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-302]]

Thus it is said. From "perceiving a self" up to "at that time they become poor": those who lack wisdom and perceive a self as true are attached to their body and the objects of their enjoyment by grasping at them as "mine." Therefore, how could those fools who do not know the suchness of things ever have the intention to give? Those who are stingy and do not give in this way will be reborn in the realm of hungry ghosts as the fruit of their maturation. Even if they emerge from there and are reborn as humans, at that time they will be poor as a result of the fruition that corresponds to the cause. ^2-302

###### Rejoicing in the generosity of others ^2-4-2-5-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-303]]

It is said: ^2-303

###### Giving with joy through being embraced by compassion ^2-4-2-5-2-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-304]]

From "at that time the bodhisattva" up to "it is not like that": at that time, knowing that sentient beings become hungry ghosts and poor because they have not practiced generosity, bodhisattvas, being seized by compassion, aspire to the intention of giving and always practice it with great generosity, without attachment. The reason for such liberality is that, having understood that all the enjoyments of the four continents, however well-adorned, are like a glob of spittle, they find joy in giving them away to others; it is not like that joy which arises from acquiring the continents after having given them to others. ^2-304

###### Embraced by skillful means ^2-4-2-5-2-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-305]]

It is said: From "the bodhisattva" up to "dedicate to the supreme," when a bodhisattva who is skilled in means and has a clear mind gives a gift, even if it is given to only one person, they think, "By the merit of giving this gift of enjoyment, may it be of benefit to all sentient beings throughout the three realms of existence," and having thought this, they give that gift. That merit of giving is then dedicated to the supreme enlightenment that is to be attained, for the sake of accomplishing the welfare of all beings. ^2-305

###### Giving with vast intent ^2-4-2-5-2-3-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-306]]

It is said: From "having given a gift" up to "seeking the excellent," furthermore, having given a gift, one does not dwell upon or become attached to the material object; the manner in which that bodhisattva never has any expectation for the maturation of the result is that all who are skilled in giving in this way give without attachment to or expectation of cause and effect. Because the power of their intention is great, even a small cause given will result in excellent, vast, and immeasurable fruit. Since there are as many sentient beings as there are in the three realms of existence—above the earth, below the earth, and upon the earth—having grasped the limit of thorough investigation, even if one were to give excellent material gifts for an infinite eon to the fields of merit—the buddhas, arhats, and pratyekabuddhas—if one then seeks the result of a śrāvaka, that is of inferior intelligence. ^2-306

###### Overwhelming others ^2-4-2-5-2-4-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-307]]

It is said: From "bodhisattva" up to "outshine," furthermore, a skilled bodhisattva, even though not engaged in material objects, possesses merit through skill in means; by rejoicing in the objects of merit of those in cyclic existence and nirvana, and by dedicating that merit arising from rejoicing as a cause for supreme, excellent enlightenment for the sake of sentient beings, through this dedication, they outshine the virtue of all beings, from the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas downwards. For example, even if there were a great heap of inferior gems, a single precious vaiḍūrya gem would outshine that entire collection of gems. Similarly, the heaps of generosity of all beings, from the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas downwards—including their vast fields, times, and material objects—are outshone by the virtue of a bodhisattva that arises from subsequent rejoicing. ^2-307

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-308]]

It is said: ^2-308

###### Gathering the merit of generosity ^2-4-2-5-4-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-309]]

From "If a bodhisattva" up to "like the waxing moon," the perfection of generosity without attachment is praised by way of an analogy: if a bodhisattva gives gifts to sentient beings, does not claim the merit of having given the gift as their own, and is without stinginess or reluctance regarding the objects to be given, then those roots of virtue arising from that non-attached generosity will increase in great power, just as the disk of the moon, possessing light and free from clouds, increases from the waxing phase onwards. Up to this point, the meaning of how to train in the six perfections of the Great Vehicle has been explained in detail and in sequence. This explains the result of training in that way; since the result of gradual cultivation arises in the continuum simultaneously, the essence of the seventh chapter—the practice of realizing full enlightenment in a single moment—is called the simultaneous cultivation of all pure phenomena, which is the ultimate, having shown the power of the intellect where the three—the basis, path, and cause of the dharmakāya—are not apprehended. As it is said: "Because the Sage, in order to perfectly gather all that is without defilement, even through each one such as generosity, realizes full enlightenment in a single moment." Therefore, here it is explained through six benefits, and according to Jñānapāda, the seventh chapter is explained by the single line "this teaching is now completed," and what follows is considered the eighth. This shows four divisions. ^2-309

#### The result of accomplishing the teachings ^2-4-3-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-310]]

It is said: "The bodhisattva..." ^2-310

##### The result of each respective stage ^2-4-3-1-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-311]]

From the phrase "of the" up to the phrase "will truly transcend," it is said: Just as has been taught above, for a bodhisattva practicing the six perfections, the generosity of a bodhisattva cuts off the continuity of birth that leads to the realm of hungry ghosts due to the lower destinies. Even if born as a human, one cuts off the continuity of all poverty of resources and, likewise, all obscurations of the afflictions. When practicing the conduct of a bodhisattva in samsara, one will attain boundless resources. By giving gifts, one gathers and brings to full maturity those sentient beings who are suffering due to deprivation. Through discipline, one abandons the nature of the many destinies of the animal realm and the eight states of non-freedom, and by that, one will always find the ten endowments and freedoms. The eight states of non-freedom are: "Hell, hungry ghosts, animals, / Barbarians, long-lived gods, / Wrong views, empty of a Buddha, / And the mute—these are the eight non-freedoms." Regarding the ten endowments, the five self-endowments are: "Born in a central land, obtained a human body, / Sense faculties complete, faith in the place, / And the finality of karma is not wrong." The five other-endowments are: "A Buddha has appeared, he has taught the Dharma, / The teaching remains for a long time, and..." It is said: [This refers to] having entered and engaged [in the path] with compassion for the sake of others. By meditating on patience that accords with the body of wisdom, one will attain a supreme, vast, and excellent form capable of benefiting others; it will be as pleasing as the color of gold, which is the example of what is pleasing to others, and one will become worthy of being looked upon. Through the diligence that promotes virtue, one’s white qualities of benefiting others will not diminish, and one will find an inexhaustible treasury of the jewel of the qualities of the ultimate victorious one and the boundless wisdom that is the fruit. Through the meditative concentration of a mind abiding in peace, because attachment to the five qualities of desire is something to be disparaged, one will abandon them, and the divine eye, the recollection of former states, the exhaustion of defilements, the knowledge of meanings, and the superknowledges and meditative concentrations will be accomplished. Through the wisdom that realizes selflessness, having thoroughly understood the nature of all phenomena as emptiness, one will truly transcend samsara, along with all sentient beings of the three realms without exception. Thus, the fruit that follows from these three causes is the meaning of the eighth chapter on the fruit. ^2-311

##### Explanation of the common results ^2-4-3-2-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-312]]

The second point, explaining the common result, is as follows: The essence of the Dharmakāya is the complete maturation of all phenomena of purification and nirvāṇa, and because it is the ultimate realization of the non-apprehension of the nature of things, it becomes the basis for the perfection of the benefit of others. As it is said:
"The Svabhāvikakāya, the Sambhogakāya,
And likewise the other, the Nirmāṇakāya,
The Dharmakāya and its activities—
These are correctly expressed as four aspects."
Thus, this passage teaches the Dharmakāya along with its activities in the manner of the four bodies. ^2-312

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-313]]

It is said: From the phrase "bull among men" up to the phrase "upholds," it refers to the bull among men who acts for the benefit of others; having attained Buddhahood and turned the precious wheel of Dharma, he then teaches the Dharma to beings for the purpose of bringing their suffering to an end. ^2-313

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-314]]

It is said: In this way, having perfected the result through listening to, contemplating, and meditating upon this teaching of the non-apprehending perfection of wisdom path, that bodhisattva, through completely pure enlightened activity, upholds the external container-world of the buddhafields and the internal essence of the pure continuum of sentient beings. They also uphold the continuum of the Buddha, the continuum of the scriptural Dharma, and the continuum of the realized Dharma; likewise, they uphold the continuum of the Sangha who practice, as well as all virtuous phenomena. With this, the explanation of the nature of the Dharma as taught by them is concluded. ^2-314

### Summary of the meaning taught by the Teacher ^2-5-0

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-315]]

It is said: From the phrase "he who acts to heal the illnesses of beings" up to the phrase "this is what he has taught," the purpose of those things mentioned above is what is called the "illnesses of beings." Because individuals who do not know this reality of non-apprehension have entered into perverted views, the omniscient teacher, who has become the supreme physician, acts to heal them by establishing them in the reality of emptiness. He has taught this, which is endowed with the profound path of complete enlightenment that teaches the wisdom of non-apprehension, and thus it has been taught correctly. With this, the nature of the six perfections, beginning with the perfection of wisdom, has been explained. ^2-315

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-316]]

It is said: It is like the precious wish-fulfilling jewel that gathers all the infinite virtues of the Buddhas of the three times. It is the authentic path of the great bodhisattvas, who have gathered all the virtues of the speech of the Tathāgatas. Furthermore, since the nature of sentient beings is primordially pure in all sentient beings universally, the Tathāgata himself has truly spoken of this path so that it may be attained by all sentient beings. ^2-316

![[bo-rangjung-dorje-tika#^2-317]]

It is said: In the *Ocean of the Perfection of Wisdom*, a commentary on the *Noble Verse Summary of the Perfection of Wisdom* which is like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel, there is the sequence for practicing the profound meaning of the supreme mind. Generally, although there are inconceivable ways of explaining this *Perfection of Wisdom* sutra, this text mostly teaches the ultimate meaning. According to the system that teaches the five perfections as auxiliary to the *perfection of wisdom*, the *perfection of wisdom* acts as a companion prior to the *perfection of generosity* and, likewise, prior to the other five perfections. According to other systems, one begins gradually from generosity, and the *perfection of wisdom* follows in a sequence of primary and secondary importance. However, here, because one cannot know how to enter into the sequence without the wisdom of the self, why is that? It is because wisdom goes first. Why is that? The *Sutra of the Precious Verse Summary of Virtues* teaches it in this general way, and specifically, it teaches that wisdom precedes the giving of generosity, and likewise for ethical discipline, patience, diligence, and meditative concentration. Thus it is explained. If one asks, "Does it not then appear after this *perfection of wisdom*?" the answer is that it does not. It arises simultaneously with the five perfections, and when considered in terms of the mode of realization, it also arises subsequently. This is the general presentation of the *perfection of wisdom*. The *Sutra of the Precious Verse Summary of the Perfection of Wisdom* is concluded. ^2-317
